UI/UX Design: For the Chores Nobody Sees

A calm home for invisible work

Nisse is a home management app for families who share a household, named after the Scandinavian house spirit that quietly keeps a home in order. It helps family members to notice, plan, remember, and appreciate the chores nobody sees.

I studied collaboration for years. Then I looked at my own home and realized I had been studying it there too. Keeping a house running is collaboration: staying somewhat fair, holding the whole picture in your head, showing up as a unit. Your neighbors know you as House number xx. You arrive at a double date as a pair. You are somebody’s mom or somebody’s pawrent. You are still yourself, but part of the time the world counts you as a team, and the work at home is what keeps that team standing. Research has a name for the hidden half of it. Cognitive household labor: the noticing, the planning, and the remembering that runs underneath the visible chores, and that lands on one partner by default. I wanted to see whether design could move it.

What existing tools get wrong

I mapped twenty products across seven kinds of tool, all built for adults sharing a home, against the two questions that decide everything: who does the noticing, and when ownership gets fixed. Four patterns fall out.

There is always an admin

Nearly every tool models a household as one owner handing work down. Whoever sets it up becomes the manager, again, and the software quietly hands them the clipboard.

We think

We think a home has no admin. The account is the household itself, not a person with a list.

Ownership gets fixed too early

One partner notices, decides it matters, then passes over the doing. A field for names turns that into a feature and calls it teamwork. The noticing never moves.

We think

We think nothing should carry a name until someone picks it up. Ownership is claimed at the moment of doing, not assigned at the moment of creation.

Pressure stands in for care

Cleaning the windows is not due Tuesday at 9:00, it is due roughly this month. Hard dates invent failure in a home that is fine, and points and leaderboards turn care into currency and partners into rivals.

We think

We think work has rhythm, not deadlines, and that fairness is a mirror rather than a scoreboard. Something to look at together, never something to win.

Automating the manager keeps the manager

The best-funded answer is to let an assistant do the noticing. But an assistant has to hand the work to somebody, so it assigns. Your partner ends up holding a notification, not a model of the home.

We think

We think the goal was never to delete the noticing. It was to let two people share it.

The core concept: a shared pool, in soft time

In Nisse, tasks are created without an owner. They sit in a shared household pool, visible to both partners, and are claimed at pickup — not assigned at creation. This one structural choice removes the delegation dynamic entirely. Nobody hands out work; nobody nags. The question shifts from “did you do what I asked” to “what does our home need, and what will I pick up.”

Recurring chores carry a rhythm (“every ~2 weeks”) instead of a deadline. Inside their window they float freely; as the window ripens, their state shifts gently. Only genuinely broken rhythms turn red — not the passing of a Tuesday. Any floating task can be promoted to a settled calendar date when life demands it: guests on Saturday, and the bathroom gets a real date. And once a month, Nisse offers a gentle retrospective of how the household’s work actually distributed itself — a conversation starter for two adults, never a verdict. The mirror shows; it doesn’t judge.

The visual system: load you can see

Abstract, organic blobs are the core visual unit. Each life-zone of the home — kitchen, bathroom, plants, deep clean — is a blob, sized by its current workload. The mental load of the household literally becomes visible area: you feel a heavy zone before you read a single word. Early explorations with literal “digital twin” floor plans were abandoned — literal representation invites literal thinking; abstraction lets size, color and state carry the meaning. Zones aren’t only rooms, either: “plants” and “deep clean” sit alongside place-blocks as equals, because that’s how the work actually feels.

The top of the home screen answers one question at a glance — how is our home doing? — with a pebble: the zone blobs nestled into one organic shape, each cell’s area reflecting its zone’s load, a qualitative phrase at the center. Never a number, never a percentage. A home doesn’t have a score.

The encoding stays small on purpose. Size is the amount of open work in a zone. A muted, dusty hue plus an icon is the zone’s identity — each color desaturated enough to coexist with every other on one screen without noise. A small dot on the blob’s edge is state: pastel for calm, amber for ripening, and red strictly reserved for genuinely overdue. Copy follows the same restraint — qualitative everywhere the product could have used a number. “Mostly calm.” “The kitchen is ripening.” The interface talks like a considerate housemate, not a project tracker. And motion is reserved for onboarding and transitions: the resting home screen is completely still, which is why these figures don’t move either.

The architecture

The whole product is two lenses over one shared pool — a spatial lens for “what does the home need,” a time lens for “what does this week look like” — plus a shopping tab, one + button, and settings that mostly govern how softly Nisse speaks. Rebuilt below from the working IA board: one node per surface, structured by what is reached from where.

Key flows, in wireframe

Round-two wireframes, drawn to settle behavior before any visual polish. Four flows carry the concept.

Capture — the field that isn’t there

One + button, three sheets: to-do, to-attend, to-buy. A new chore gets a name, a zone, and an optional rhythm — and that’s all. The most important form field in the app is the one that doesn’t exist: there is no “assign to.” Work is born ownerless, into the shared pool.

A chore’s life — rhythm, not deadline

Every chore carries a rhythm card — “every 7–12 days, ripens around day 9” — instead of a due date. Mark it done, undo it, backdate it (“when did this actually happen?” keeps the rhythm honest), or plan it out to a day and a part of day. Red appears only when a rhythm is genuinely broken.

The week — promoting is a gesture

The time lens mixes settled dates, busy blocks and floating chores in one week stream. Drag a floating chore onto a day to plan it; drag it onto a past day to log it done after the fact. Promotion is a gesture, not a form — and releasing a chore returns it to its window.

Household & takk — recognition, not points

The household trail shows who did what — attribution without a scoreboard. And takk (Norwegian for “thanks”) lets one partner stamp a little gratitude on any deed; it drops softly onto the other’s phone. The app gets to deliver good news, not only complaints.

Quiet by settings, too

The settings surface is mostly about restraint: how Nisse speaks up (gentle — one pulse a day · quiet — weekly digest · off), quiet hours, away mode. The monthly recap lives here as reflection, not gamification — “June: the house hummed along; most loved blob: yard.” Deletion always explains recovery, and “recently deleted” keeps everything for 30 days.

What didn’t make it

The rejected directions defined the product as much as the accepted ones.

Silent windows

Tasks stayed invisible inside their cadence window and only surfaced when attention was needed. It created a purely negative feedback loop — the app only ever spoke to complain. Now status is always visible, and mostly it’s visibly fine.

“Only spoke to complain.”

Arc rings & geometric indicators

Every iteration toward neat, thin, mechanical shapes — progress rings, puzzle layouts — got pulled back. Geometry reads as measurement; measurement reads as judgment. The correct direction, every time, was looser and more pebble-like.

“Geometry reads as judgment.”

External halos for state

State began as a glow around each blob — too loud at pebble scale; the composition started shouting. State moved to a small dot on the blob’s edge: legible up close, silent from afar.

“The composition started shouting.”

The literal home

Realistic room representations made the app feel like a facilities dashboard. Abstract blobs made it feel like a living thing.

“A facilities dashboard, not a living thing.”

Removing “assign to” did more for fairness than any fairness feature could. And restraint, it turns out, has to be designed repeatedly — calm isn’t a default you set once but a choice you re-make at every screen, against every instinct to add a number, a badge, a pulse of motion. Qualitative beats quantitative in emotional territory: a home described as “mostly calm” starts a different conversation than a home scored 73%. And abstraction is honest — blobs don’t pretend to know your house; they only claim to know how much it’s asking of you right now.

The same lessons apply to the way it was built. Working with Claude Code didn’t lower the bar for design judgment — it raised the price of not having any. Next: treatment explorations of the pulse pebble, a full prototype of the claim-and-promote gestures, and living with the fairness mirror through a real monthly cycle with real couples — because a mirror can only be judged after a household has actually looked into it.

If this made you think about the invisible work in your own home — that’s the point.
